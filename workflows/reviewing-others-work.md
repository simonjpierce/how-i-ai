# Reviewing someone else's manuscript

When you're asked to review a paper — a student's thesis chapter, a collaborator's draft, a co-authored manuscript — produce a thorough, genuinely independent review *and* hand the author a clear, plain-language note they can actually use, instead of a few scribbled margin notes.

This is an idea file. Paste it into Claude Code to build a version for your own work; it'll fill in the specifics (folder names, exact tools) in collaboration with you. (It has to be an agent that can run tools and create files on your machine — a plain chat assistant can't wire up the review passes or fetch the document.)

*What you'll need: the second, independent reviewer — the heart of this — needs a second AI model reachable on the command line (the maintainer uses the Codex CLI), which is a separate install and account. With only one model you can still get a fresh, hostile-prompted pass in a clean context, but weight it less.*

## The core idea

Reviewing is the mirror image of [drafting your own work](./writing-and-review.md): there, a different model attacks *your* draft before a reader does; here, you *are* the reader, and the job is to attack someone else's draft fairly, then give them something genuinely useful back. Two failure modes spoil that. One: a single AI pass is one opinion — confident, but unchecked. Two: the output reads like a machine — a wall of terse findings an early-career author finds discouraging rather than helpful.

The fix is four moves:

- **Review it more than once, independently.** Two or three different AI models read the whole manuscript separately — your main model, a second model on the command line, and a third where you have one — at least one of them especially good at statistics and at catching numbers that don't add up. Where they *agree*, you have a strong signal; where they differ, something to weigh. Two independent readers beat one; three from different model families is better still.
- **Verify the numbers yourself.** AI reviewers are good at spotting that a total doesn't match a table, but a note going to a co-author must not assert a wrong figure. So you re-check each number the review leans on — re-sum the column, re-count the rows — before it goes in.
- **Check the inference, not just the prose.** A separate pass asks whether the *analysis itself* is sound, not just whether the arithmetic adds up: are repeated measurements on the same animal treated as independent (pseudoreplication)? How many things were tested versus reported? Is a "no effect" really just too small a sample to tell? Does a label like "resident" claim more than the data show? These inferential problems are higher-stakes than any wording fix — a paper can be perfectly typeset and still be drawing the wrong conclusion.
- **Send the author only what you've verified.** The full synthesis stays with you. The author gets a short note, addressed to them by name, that carries just the things you've confirmed are wrong — factual errors and citations that don't support their sentence — each paired with a concrete fix and the check behind it. Clear, direct and polite, jargon explained. It's an assessment, not a pep talk — openly the output of an assistant, never pretending to be you, and never imitating a mentor's reassurance.

## How it runs

**Get the manuscript into a clean, readable form first.** If the document is shared with you, a proper connection to your Google account (or Google's own command-line tool, for a document you can edit) can usually pull the full text directly — and the command-line route also brings who commented and what changed. Check you actually got the body, not just the page furniture. Only when those routes fail, fall back to asking for a plain Word copy and converting it to clean text your agent can read in full.

**Run the reviewers.** One is your main model, reading at full effort and bringing what it knows about the field, the site, the prior literature. The other one or two are *different* models on the command line, run **read-only** so they can comment but can't touch the file, each told to be a rigorous-but-supportive journal reviewer: for every issue, give the location, the problem, how serious it is, and a concrete fix; end with the few highest-value priorities and a genuine list of strengths. Run them concurrently — while they think, write up the first.

**Check the arithmetic.** Take every count, total, and "X says one thing, Y says another" the review surfaced, and confirm it against the actual document yourself. When a number only exists as a bar or point in a figure with no accompanying table, don't take the caption's word for it — calibrate against the axis and measure the value directly from the image before treating it as confirmed. State the confirmed ones as *verified*, not "the AI thinks". This is what lets you stand behind the note. It also cuts the other way: a catch only one reviewer made isn't dropped for that reason — if you can confirm it yourself (a sentence that contradicts the one before it, a total that doesn't add up), it goes in at its full weight.

**Check the inference, not just the sums.** Add an explicit pass — your own, or a reviewer prompted specifically for it — on whether the *analysis* is valid: pseudoreplication, multiplicity (how many things were tested versus reported), conclusions a small sample can't support, labels that over-reach. Three more questions belong here. How much data did an exclusion throw away — records identified only to genus, unusable footage — and would analysing at a coarser level (genus or family) keep them? For each ecological interpretation, and for each absence, rarity or seasonal low, has the plainest *sampling-design* explanation been considered first, before reading it as animal behaviour or differences in how species use resources? That means when sampling happened (daytime-only, a single season, operator closures or quiet booking months), where (one habitat), what the recorder chose to record (guides film what interests them, so common or cryptic species go under-recorded), and how detectable the species is (shy, deep, cryptic). Where only the people who collected the data can say which applies, write the question for them. And does the paper deliver what its title and aims promise its readers — a paper framed as a method or a case study needs the transferable practicalities: how the data were collected, shared and processed, and what it asked of the people who supplied them. AI reviewers tend to lead with surface fixes unless you point them straight at inferential validity, so make it its own step. It's usually the highest-value thing the whole review produces.

**Add a house-conventions pass.** Run the draft past your field's manuscript conventions — the same ones you'd apply to [your own science writing](./the-science-workflow.md): citation density (don't stack eight references on one claim), where the discussion should open, what an abstract has to carry (a compact, quantitative mini-paper that still reads for a non-specialist — not an over-long methods digest), calibrating claims to the evidence, keeping interpretation in the captions rather than on the figures themselves. This catches craft issues the correctness passes don't.

**Show *how*, not just *what* — give method fixes a worked example.** The most useful thing a review can do with a methods criticism is hand over a recipe. When a fix recommends a *different* analysis or method, don't stop at naming it — find two or three published papers that applied that method in a comparable setting and offer them as templates: "here are a few papers that did something similar; see which fits your case, and how they did it." Present them as candidates to check, *not* verified-perfect citations — a near-exact match is often hard to find, and giving a few lets the reader pick the closest. Verify each is real and actually uses the method (a second model reading the candidates' methods sections is good for this — expect it to reject roughly half of a first list, so have replacements ready and re-check those too), and group them in the full findings by which fix they support. A criticism you can act on by following a worked example is worth far more than one you're left to solve alone. This leans on having your reference library searchable as full text (see [the reference library](./the-reference-library.md)), with a web search as the fallback when your own library has no close fit. The same library search applies wherever the paper claims a knowledge gap, "few studies", or a first record: check for relevant work it doesn't cite at that claim, and note it as a suggestion to confirm.

**Write two documents: a short note for the author, and the full findings for you.** Combine everything into a single synthesis — don't staple three reviews together — and keep it as your own internal record: all the passes merged, the three severity groups (what a journal reviewer will likely pick up, what would strengthen the paper, minor or stylistic points), the working for each number, method recommendations with their worked examples, and the strengths. If you are a co-author on the paper and will write your own comments, the internal file opens with a one-screen list for your own read: claims that run ahead of the data, terms that imply a design the study didn't have, and the sampling questions only the data collectors can answer. That list never goes in the author note. What the author receives is much shorter, because authors who don't use AI tend to skim past a heavy review. The note carries only what you've verified yourself and can show: factual errors (numbers that don't match the paper's own tables, wrong names or figure references, a reported statistic that doesn't fit the stated method, wording that states something false), citations that don't support the sentence they're attached to (quoting what the cited paper actually says), breaches of the target journal's hard requirements, and errors in the reference list. Anything a co-author has already raised in the shared document or by email stays out of the note and goes in the internal file. Each item gets its exact location, the fix, and the check behind it — for a number, the working the author can re-run by hand. Opinion, framing, prose style, "consider adding" suggestions, re-analysis recommendations and anything you couldn't verify stay in the internal file, as does anything that would send the author off to collect more data. Group the items by kind with a short at-a-glance summary at the top, aim for about a page, explain every technical term in plain words, and end with a single line of caveat. The note must also stand entirely on its own: complete in itself, with no mention of earlier notes, versions, passes or "what's new", no models or process, and no naming of which co-author's wording introduced an error. That holds on a re-review too — rebuild the whole note, with earlier items re-checked against the current text, rather than sending only what's changed; the comparison with the previous round is for you, in the internal file. You decide whether any of the full findings goes further — for example to a collaborator who works with their own AI tool and would rather feed it the whole review. If a shareable link is useful, the note can be published to a web page that isn't listed or indexed by search engines — but anyone with the link can read it, so it isn't private. Only ever on request, and knowing it discusses unpublished work. And if the manuscript arrived as a Word file, the standalone note isn't your only option: you can also return a tracked-changes-and-comments marked-up copy of *their* document. It carries the same verified items as the note and nothing from the internal file — clean corrections as inline edits the author accepts or rejects one by one, check-this items as margin comments — in the tool they're already working in.

## What this does *not* do

It doesn't send anything as you, and it never writes *as* you — it's openly an assistant's review, so the author knows exactly what they're reading. It doesn't replace the author's supervisors or co-authors, or your own read — it's the pass that gets the obvious errors fixed before those people spend their time. It won't decide what's safe to publish (a sensitive location, an unpublished result) — it flags; you judge. And two models agreeing is a strong signal, not a journal panel's verdict.

## Why this works

A single review is one opinion you can't easily calibrate. Two independent models, plus you checking the numbers, turns it into something you can actually stand behind — and the convergence between them tells you which findings are solid. Cutting what reaches the author down to the verified items is not decoration: a short note that is specific, polite and checkable gets acted on, where a heavy review gets skimmed or defended against — and one padded with reassurance gets skimmed too. You end up giving a frank, accurate read with the fixes attached, while the fuller analysis stays with you to use as you judge, at a scale you couldn't manage by hand.

## Note

This is a pattern, not a fixed tool. How adversarial you make the reviewers, how many you run, whether you add the conventions pass, the worked-example recipes for method fixes, the marked-up Word copy, or the publish step — all yours, and all optional. The publish-to-a-link step only matters if you have someone to send it to; drop it otherwise. It assumes your main agent can reach a second model for the independent pass — that's the one piece worth setting up.

The mirror case — reviewing *your own* draft before submission — is its own workflow: [reviewing your own manuscript](./reviewing-your-own-manuscript.md). The same multi-model pass and number-checking apply, but instead of a note for someone else you fold the fixes straight into your text, and add a [replication audit](./the-replication-audit.md) (is the code and data behind every figure and number actually in a repository someone could re-run?). Decide up front whose manuscript this is, because it changes the output (a short note *for* an author, versus edits *into* your own draft).

The durable idea is: *review it independently more than once, verify the numbers and the inference yourself, then give the author only the verified fixes, openly as an assistant, never as you.* Paste this to your AI and shape it to the kind of work you review.
