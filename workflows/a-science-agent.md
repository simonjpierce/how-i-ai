# A science agent

Give your research its own standing assistant: one that knows every project you have running, where each one stands, the rules your analyses follow and the mistakes it has made before, so each research session starts from that knowledge instead of from zero.

This is an idea file. Paste it into your AI agent — Claude Code or Codex — to build a version for your own work; it'll fill in the specifics (folder names, exact tools) in collaboration with you. (It has to be an agent that can create files on your machine — a plain chat assistant can't set this up.)

## The core idea

[The science workflow](./the-science-workflow.md) makes a *single* analysis rigorous: a lab notebook written as you go, and a second model checking each step. But research is rarely one analysis. It's a thesis chapter, two papers at different stages, a co-author's draft you owe comments on, and a dataset someone else is still cleaning. Without something holding the whole set, each session starts cold: it doesn't know the paper is waiting on a supervisor, that a method was tried and abandoned last month, or that this dataset belongs to a partner and must never leave the laptop.

The fix is a **science agent**: a short, standing set of files that every research session reads before it starts, plus a habit of writing back what each session learns. It isn't a separate program. It's a role defined in files: what it's for, the rules it follows, what each project is and where it stands, and a growing record of the corrections you've made. The principle it rests on is the same as the rest of this collection: **the knowledge lives in files, not in anyone's memory**, so a fresh session on the hundredth day is as well briefed as the one that's been running all morning.

## The pieces

**The brief.** One file, read by every research session: what the science agent is for (moving research to finished, defensible outputs), what it leaves to you (the question, which method suits the biology, what a result means, what goes to co-authors), and the standing rules. Keep it to a few screens; detail lives elsewhere.

**The stage ladder.** Each project records its stage: *question → data → analysis → writing → co-author review → submitted → in review → revision → accepted → published.* Progress means a stage moving, not "worked on it", and a stage moves only on evidence: a journal's email, a co-author saying it's gone, your word. If a session *thinks* a stage has changed, it asks; it never assumes, because a wrong "accepted" can set off a funder thank-you or a social post about a paper that hasn't been accepted.

**Stalled, or a big job?** The most useful judgement call the agent makes. A paper can sit still for two reasons: it's blocked on a small task (do it, or hand it to a session), or it needs a large, indivisible piece of work, like a week of writing or a long analysis run. The second needs a block of time in the calendar, not reminders. The maintainer's agent once flagged a long review paper as "stalled near submission" for weeks; it was in fact an 85,000-word draft that needed a multi-day condensing job, and the reminders were pure noise.

**Project notes with data rules.** One note per project: what it is, its stage and next step, the people involved, its decisions (dated, with the reason), a link to its lab notebook, and **who owns the data and where it may go.** That last line matters most when you work with partners: footage from a dive operator, a dataset under a sharing agreement, records from another lab. The agent reads it before it commits, uploads or shares anything, and large raw files (video, audio, images) stay off code-hosting sites regardless.

**The rules that have cost the most to learn.** The brief carries the analysis rules worth stating every time. The [science workflow](./the-science-workflow.md) covers them in full; in short: the biology and the question come before the model, and the simplest model comes first; raw inputs are traced back and spot-checked before anything is fitted; time zones are confirmed before files are compared; what a file contains is checked rather than read off its name (a file named "updated April" turned out to hold data only to December); numbers from published papers are checked against the source rather than treated as targets to reproduce; existing published code is extended rather than rebuilt; tables are generated by script, never typed by hand; and dead ends are written down and read before the next method is proposed.

**Known failure modes.** A short list, kept in the brief, of the ways *this agent* has gone wrong before, so every session watches for them. The maintainer's list includes: reaching for an over-complex model before the simple one was tried; treating a published estimate as the answer to match; blurring two related methods when the task named one; designing a whole methodology when the task was only to set up a tool; and inventing a label nobody asked for. Your list will be different; start it the first time something goes wrong twice.

**When to stop and ask.** Named in the brief, so it isn't left to the agent's judgement in the moment: any population or statistical estimate before it is stated as a result; any text going to a co-author, supervisor or journal (always a draft you send yourself); any expensive run (hours of computing, thousands of images), whose design is agreed and written down first; and any change to data you don't own.

**Corrections become rules.** When you or a co-author correct how something was done, the correction goes into a rules file in your words, with the date, and every later session reads it. This is how the agent stops repeating a mistake, and it's the part that compounds: after a few months the rules file holds a large part of how you actually do science.

## How it runs

In practice the science agent isn't usually its own conversation. Each research project gets its own session (see [running many sessions at once](./running-many-sessions-at-once.md)), and each of those reads the brief, the rules file and its project note before starting. Once there's enough research work to need a view across it, add a standing "science" session that walks the project notes once a week: which stage each project is at, what's stalled and why, which lab notebooks have code changes newer than their last entry, and what's owed to co-authors. It reports the one thing that most needs you.

**Seeding one for a student.** A supervisor can give a student a head start by writing the general brief from their own agent (the stage ladder, the analysis rules, the failure modes) and leaving out their own projects and private material. The student's agent reads that brief, and the student's own rules file sits on top of it and wins wherever they differ. Kept in a shared repository that the student pulls, it improves for both of them over time, and nobody has to email files back and forth.

## What this does *not* do

It doesn't do your science for you. The research question, the choice of method, what a result means, and what goes to co-authors stay with you; the agent proposes, with its reasons, and writes down what you decide. It doesn't replace the per-step checks in [lab-notebook analysis](./lab-notebook-analysis.md); it's the layer above them, keeping a whole portfolio of analyses rigorous and moving. And it doesn't chase people: it tells you what's owed and drafts the message, and you decide whether to send it.

## Why this works

Most research mistakes aren't new mistakes: they're the same handful, repeated because the lesson lived in someone's head and that session was a different one. Writing the brief, the failure modes and the corrections into files that every session reads turns each lesson into a rule the next session follows. The stage ladder and the data rules do the same for the portfolio: they keep "where is this paper?" and "may this data leave the laptop?" from depending on memory.

## Note

This is a pattern, not a fixed implementation. The parts that are yours to shape: the stages that fit your field, how many projects need a standing session, what goes in the rules file, and how far to take the weekly review. The durable idea is: *one standing brief, project notes that carry their own data rules, and every correction written down as a rule, so each research session starts already knowing what the last one learned.* Paste this to your AI and build the version that fits how you work.
