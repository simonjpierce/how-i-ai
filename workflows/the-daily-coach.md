# The daily coach

A way to turn "I have a hundred things to do and can't make myself start any of them" into "do this one thing — now this one — now this one," so the day actually moves instead of dissolving into overwhelm and a still-full inbox.

This is an idea file. Paste it into your AI agent — Claude Code or Codex — to build a version for your own work; it'll fill in the specifics in collaboration with you. (It has to be an agent that can create files on your machine — a plain chat assistant can't set this up.)

## The core idea

A to-do list is a pile of everything, all visible at once, with no guidance on what to touch first. For a lot of people — especially anyone who fights procrastination or overwhelm — that pile is *paralysing*: the cost of deciding what to do next is itself enough to make you do none of it, or scatter across five half-things, or quietly avoid the one that matters. The list grows, the email backs up, and the gap between "what I meant to do today" and "what I did" becomes its own source of dread.

The move here is to stop treating your day as a list to stare at and start treating it as something an AI **coaches you through, one item at a time.** Two beats:

- **Plan the day.** Each morning the AI pulls together your real inputs — calendar, tasks, what's due, what you flagged last night, what's still open from yesterday — and produces a *short, ordered* list of what actually matters today. Not everything. The few things that move the needle, in the order to do them. The plan can be waiting for you: the maintainer's is built by a scheduled job before he's up, and a second model checks it against the sources before it opens. Work a scheduled job did overnight isn't summarised for you: the plan shows only a draft waiting for your review and send, or an input only you can supply, plus one line for any job you approved that failed to deliver, so a silent failure can't pass as a quiet night. An approval stands until its job has run, so approved jobs that haven't had a night yet get one shared "still waiting" line rather than one each.
- **Walk it, one task at a time.** Then it surfaces *one* task — just the next one — and helps you actually start it: the concrete first action, the file already open, the draft already begun. You do it (or consciously skip it), it marks it done, and *only then* does the next one appear. You're never looking at the whole pile, only at the single thing in front of you.

You can run more than one coached block a day. The maintainer runs two: a morning block for the main job, and a shorter, more targeted afternoon block for the work the main job would otherwise crowd out — which is how that second kind of work gets done at all. The morning has its own usual shape. It opens with any replies owed to the few relationships that matter most — board members, major donors and funders, live fundraising leads — because diving into deep work while those wait can itself be a form of procrastination; if you head into deep work with some still waiting, the coach says so once, and you choose. Then the most important piece of deep work runs until about noon, with a coaching nudge around noon that you can decline, rather than a hard stop. From noon come two dedicated hours of what the maintainer calls management mode, one for each of two areas of work that need regular attention. Each is served one task at a time: the most important thing you can do in that area in an hour, which may be that area's ready emails and drafts, checked and sent with their context, or other work from the area's priorities, and the next only if time remains. The second of those hours opens with one small item from a standing list of jobs being chipped away at, about ten minutes, with the page and a prepared draft already open; a skipped day serves the same item next time, never two. If the morning's deep work was itself a project in one of those areas, it counts as that area's hour, and an hour on governance and strategy takes its place instead; anything dated that the replaced hour would have held stays on the plan as its own line. The afternoon normally begins when those hours end, not at a fixed clock time; a fixed calendar commitment, or simply choosing to move on, brings it forward.

The morning plan also looks ahead at the calendar. The maintainer first had a brief built automatically before every meeting, and dropped it. What he is moving to is narrower: an evening job, set up but not yet proven in daily use, is meant to build a brief — who's attending, what your notes and recent email say about them, what you promised last time, and a few points to raise — only for a short list of standing meetings he names, and link it from that day's calendar line. Anything else gets one built on request, so he chooses when it's worth having.

The same pattern clears your inbox. Instead of "deal with email" (vague, infinite, avoidable), the AI surfaces the messages waiting on a reply and sorts them by importance: the few that matter today join the morning plan, and the rest get walked down later with a visible countdown, ten to zero. For each one it reads the whole thread, then shows who the sender is and how the contact came about, plus a checklist of everything they asked for, each marked done, open (and whose job it is), or a decision for you, and asks how you want to handle it; only then does it draft the reply, for you to edit. Drafts are cut to the length the reply actually needs before you see them, and any attachments are listed by name. Once you approve a reply, it is placed in the email thread itself as a draft, so sending is a single press from you rather than a copy-and-paste. Every approved draft waiting in your email is also gathered into one ready-to-send list, so none sits forgotten in a drafts folder: in the management-mode hours the coach serves it one row at a time (you send it, hold it with a reason, or drop it), and if you say "later", it offers whatever is left once more in the early afternoon, as long as the coaching session is still open. A finite, finishable thing with a defined finish line. A send counts only when the message really carries the Sent label — a message id alone could be an unsent draft — and the coach checks that itself rather than asking you whether a reply has gone. A thread isn't counted as settled just because the other person closed it: before dropping one, the AI checks your plan, your task list and your notes for something you still owe them, such as a promised set of comments, and keeps the thread if so. Threads that really are settled are then archived out of the inbox. Archiving is reversible, and each one is logged with how to undo it, so the inbox shrinks without anything being lost.

## How it runs

**The plan is built from your stuff, not invented.** The AI reads the inputs you already keep — your calendar, your task manager, your notes — and proposes the day; you're steering, not accepting a stranger's idea of your priorities. Before it presents an action as still to do, it checks that against the evidence — a verified send, for example, retires an old "send this" prompt — so finished work doesn't come back as unfinished. **A later conversation updates the plan.** If you talk the day through after the plan was built (a voice walk, say), the coach merges that in before it starts coaching: it reads the whole debrief and transcripts, not just a summary, takes the order you gave, and rewrites the plan's opening and its start-here line. Anything the conversation already drafted is linked rather than redone, so the plan never sends you to rewrite something that already exists. Questions the conversation left open are attached to the plan line they block and asked when the coach reaches that line, never replayed as a wall of walk answers before you've started real work. **The day itself can update the plan too.** On request, and without starting any coaching, the AI gathers what actually happened — the meetings held and their notes, mail sent and received, chat — and rewrites the plan's opening as a "today so far" view: how the day went, what is still unsent, what each meeting changed and the new actions it produced, and a recommendation for tomorrow. The morning's version is kept beneath it unchanged, and items the day changed get a short factual note. **One thing is "live" at a time.** The coach holds the rest of the list in reserve and shows you only the current task, with whatever it takes to lower the activation cost of starting — the first sentence written, the link opened, the question framed. **The most important thing is guarded.** The plan names one piece of work that matters most today, and the coach starts there or gets to it early. If an earlier item begins to sprout extra work beyond its real-world step — a new system to build, a new tool to set up — the coach says so as it happens and offers a choice: finish the real step now and park the build until after the important task (its recommendation), or keep building. And if a good part of the morning has gone without the important task starting, it names that in a line before taking anything new on. **Nothing quietly falls off.** Whatever you didn't finish yesterday isn't shunted into a "leftovers" pile below the list. It competes with today's new work for a place in the plan, with how long it has been waiting shown beside it, and it leaves only when it's done or you drop it. A small check compares yesterday's open items with the new plan and lists anything the plan left out, so an omission is visible and can be reversed, never silent. A carried-forward date also keeps its owner and origin: a date the AI set for its own draft or prep is never restated as your deadline to someone else, or called overdue to them, and where the origin is unclear the date is dropped and flagged as unverified. **Completion is explicit and a little bit rewarded.** Each finished item is marked and counted; the inbox pass literally counts down to zero. The finish line is the point — crossing it is the reward, so it isn't buried. **Stopping early is fine.** If you get through six of ten, that's six done, not four failed — the framing never turns into guilt.

## What this does *not* do

It doesn't do the work for you, and it doesn't decide what matters *instead* of you — you can always reorder, skip, or veto, and the priorities come from your own inputs. It also isn't a nag or a tracker that judges you: the value is in *removing the friction of deciding, starting, and keeping count*, not in surveillance. And it won't send anything on your behalf — the inbox pass drafts replies, and once you approve one it sits as a draft in the thread, but you read it and press send yourself. Replies drafted during coaching only reach your email after you approve them. Separately, an overnight job can place a small set of routine drafts that pass its checks, such as donor thank-yous, in their threads for you to look over. Either way you read each one and press send yourself, and the early-morning planning runs never create any drafts.

## Why this works

The active ingredient is the *one-at-a-time constraint.* Overwhelm is largely the cost of holding the whole list in your head plus the decision of what to do next; removing both — by showing exactly one chosen thing — is what makes starting possible. And a defined, finite finish line ("ten down to zero", "the five that matter today", "an hour on the thing I'm furthest behind on") converts an infinite, avoidable obligation into a bounded, finishable one. None of this requires willpower you don't have; it restructures the task so less willpower is needed.

## Note

This is a pattern, not a fixed implementation. What you feed the planner, whether you run one coached block or two, an email countdown, an evening review, or just one of them, how much "help me start" each task gets — all yours, and all optional. The durable idea is: *don't make yourself face the whole pile; have the AI plan from your real inputs and then put exactly one chosen thing in front of you at a time, with a finish line you can actually reach.* Paste this to your AI and build the version that fits how you work.
